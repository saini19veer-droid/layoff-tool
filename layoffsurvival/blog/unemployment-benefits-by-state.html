Unemployment Benefits by State 2026: What You'll Actually Receive

The difference between the highest and lowest paying states is nearly $700 per week. Here's what your state will actually pay you.

LayoffSurvival — July 2026 — 8 min read

Unemployment insurance is one of the most valuable financial resources available after a layoff — and one of the least understood. Most people have a vague sense that it exists and pays "some percentage" of their salary. The actual mechanics are more specific, more variable, and more important to understand than that.

Here's what you actually need to know.

How the weekly benefit amount is calculated

Every state uses its own formula, but most fall into one of two approaches: a percentage of your quarterly wages, or a percentage of your weekly wage. The most common formula is roughly 40–60% of your average weekly wage, up to a state-specific maximum.

The maximum weekly benefit is where states diverge dramatically. Massachusetts pays up to $1,033 per week (one of the highest in the US). Mississippi pays up to $235 per week (the lowest). If you've been earning $100,000 a year, your benefit is capped by your state's maximum — not by your actual wage percentage.

How to estimate your specific benefit

The rough calculation for most states: take your highest-earning quarter in the last 18 months, divide by 26, and multiply by 0.5. Then check if that number exceeds your state's weekly maximum — if it does, you receive the maximum instead.

Example: You earned $22,000 in your highest quarter. $22,000 ÷ 26 × 0.5 = $423/week. If your state maximum is $500, you receive $423. If your state maximum is $350, you receive $350.

File immediately — every day matters Most states have a one-week waiting period before benefits start — but that clock begins on the day you file, not the day you were laid off. Every day you delay filing is a day of benefits permanently lost. File the same day as your layoff or the following morning.

How long benefits last

Standard unemployment benefits last 26 weeks in most states. But several states have cut this — Florida goes as low as 12 weeks depending on the state unemployment rate, and North Carolina, Georgia, and a few others have variable durations tied to economic conditions.

Extended benefits may be available during periods of high unemployment, triggered automatically when state unemployment rates exceed certain thresholds. These are not always available and depend entirely on economic conditions at the time of your claim.

What disqualifies you or reduces your benefits

You must have been laid off through no fault of your own. If you were fired for cause or resigned voluntarily, you do not qualify. Severance is treated differently by state — some states count it as wages and delay the start of your benefits, others allow you to collect unemployment alongside severance immediately.

Side income matters: if you earn money while collecting unemployment — freelance work, part-time jobs — you must report it. Most states reduce your benefit by the amount earned over a small disregard threshold. This doesn't mean you shouldn't work — partial benefits are better than no benefits — but you must report accurately or risk repayment demands later.

Taxes on unemployment benefits

Unemployment benefits are taxable as ordinary income at the federal level. Most states also tax them. You can request voluntary withholding (10% federal) when you enroll. If you don't withhold, you'll owe the taxes when you file — which can be a painful surprise if you've spent all the benefits by April.

Request the withholding. It reduces your weekly check slightly but eliminates a tax bill at the worst possible time.
