How Long Will My Money Last After a Layoff?

Most people guess at this number. Here's the actual math — and why it's almost always different from what people expect.

LayoffSurvival — July 2026 — 7 min read

When you get laid off, this is the question that matters more than any other: how long can I actually last?

The answer determines whether you can afford to be selective about your next job, whether you need to cut your budget immediately, whether you can take time to retrain or freelance, and how much urgency your job search actually requires.

Most people guess at this number. They think "I have some savings, a few months of severance, I'll be fine." Then six months later they're in a much tighter spot than they expected because they didn't do the math on a few things that matter a lot.

The four components of your runway

Your financial runway is the sum of four things divided by your monthly expenses:

  • Liquid savings — checking, savings accounts, money market. Not retirement accounts, not home equity.
  • Net severance — your severance amount after taxes. Severance is taxed as ordinary income, typically withheld at 22% federal plus state. A $20,000 severance grosses about $14,000–$15,000 after tax in most states.
  • Unemployment benefits — most states pay 40–60% of your prior weekly wage up to a state cap, for 12–26 weeks. This is real money that most people undercount.
  • Other liquid assets — any taxable brokerage accounts, CDs maturing soon, or other accessible cash.

The expenses number is where most people get it wrong

People consistently underestimate their monthly expenses by 20–30% when doing this math in their head. The categories they miss:

  • Health insurance — if you were on employer-sponsored insurance, you now need to pay this yourself. COBRA averages $685/month individual, $1,950/month family. This is the single biggest expense change after a layoff for most people.
  • Annual expenses divided by 12 — car registration, insurance renewals, Amazon Prime, any annual subscriptions. These feel invisible until they hit.
  • Income taxes on unemployment — unemployment benefits are taxable income. If you don't withhold voluntarily, you'll owe at tax time. Factor this in.
The COBRA shock The single most common mistake in runway calculations is forgetting to add health insurance costs. If you were on a family plan through your employer, going from $300/month employee contribution to $1,950/month COBRA full premium is a $1,650/month difference. On a $4,800/month expense budget, that's a 34% increase that immediately shrinks your runway significantly.

How to extend your runway without finding a new job

Every dollar you reduce in monthly expenses extends your runway proportionally. On $4,800/month expenses, cutting $800/month extends an 8-month runway to over 9.5 months — almost two extra months without changing anything about your income side.

The cuts that actually move the needle:

  • Choose ACA marketplace over COBRA if the cost difference is significant — can save $400–$1,500/month
  • Pause or cancel streaming, gym, and subscription services — typically $150–$400/month
  • Reduce dining out to near-zero — average American household spends $600–$900/month on restaurants, easily cut to $100–$150
  • Defer any large planned purchases until you have income again
