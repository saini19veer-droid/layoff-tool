What to Do in the First 48 Hours After a Layoff

You just got the call. Here's the order that matters — because some of these have hard deadlines you don't know about yet.

LayoffSurvival — July 2026 — 8 min read

The first thing most people do after a layoff is nothing. They sit with the shock, call family, maybe have a drink. That's completely human and completely understandable.

But there are three financial decisions in the first 48 hours that have real deadlines attached to them. Miss them and you lose money — sometimes thousands of dollars. Here's what they are and exactly when to handle each one.

Hour 1–3: Get your paperwork before your access gets cut

Most companies cut system access on the same day as the layoff, sometimes within hours. Before that happens — or if you have any window at all — download everything that's yours:

  • Your pay stubs for the last 12 months (you'll need these for unemployment)
  • Any offer letters, promotion letters, or compensation agreements
  • Your benefits summary — what health coverage you have and when it ends
  • Any unvested equity schedule if you have stock options or RSUs
  • Contact information for colleagues you'll want as references

If your access is already cut, don't panic. Your last pay stubs are available through your state's employer records, and your W-2 will arrive in January. But getting them now saves weeks of friction later.

Day 1: Calculate your runway before you make any other decision

Before you decide whether to take COBRA, before you figure out your job search strategy, before you do anything else — you need to know one number: how many months can you survive without income?

This number determines everything. If your runway is 3 months, you need a job offer fast and cannot be selective. If your runway is 9 months, you can take time to find the right role. If it's 18+ months, you potentially have the option to take a break, retrain, or start something.

The calculation is: (savings + severance + projected unemployment benefits) ÷ monthly expenses. Most people underestimate their monthly expenses by 20–30% when doing this math quickly. Be honest about subscriptions, insurance, food, and utilities.

Day 1: File for unemployment — today, not next week

This is the one with the most expensive deadline. Every state has a waiting period before your first unemployment payment — typically one week. That waiting period clock starts when you file, not when you got laid off. Every day you delay filing is a day of benefits you don't get back.

On $70,000 annual salary, your weekly unemployment benefit might be $450–$650 depending on state. One week of delay costs you that amount. Two weeks costs you double. File the day you're laid off, or the next morning at the latest.

Go directly to your state's unemployment agency website — not a third-party site. You'll need your employer's name and address, your dates of employment, and your last day of work. Most states process online applications in under 20 minutes.

Important If you received severance, it may affect when your unemployment benefits start depending on your state. Some states treat severance as wages and delay the start of benefits. This varies significantly by state — check your specific state's rules when you file.

Day 2: Make your COBRA decision — you have 60 days, but don't wait

COBRA lets you continue your employer's health insurance for up to 18 months after a layoff. The catch: you pay 100–102% of the full premium — both your share and what your employer was previously covering.

The average COBRA cost in 2026 is $685/month for individual coverage and $1,950/month for family coverage. That's a serious number. But the alternative — an ACA marketplace plan — may be significantly cheaper, especially if your income has dropped substantially.

You have 60 days from your layoff to elect COBRA and 60 days to enroll in an ACA marketplace plan (a layoff is a qualifying life event). But don't wait 60 days to research this. Healthcare coverage should not lapse. Start comparing costs now.

The decision comes down to: if you have ongoing medical needs, in-network doctors you need to keep, or a deductible you've already met this year — COBRA is often worth the premium. If you're generally healthy and your income has dropped, an ACA plan with subsidies may cost significantly less.

Day 2: Review your severance agreement carefully before signing

If you were given a severance agreement, you almost certainly have more time to sign than you think. Federal law (the Older Workers Benefit Protection Act) gives workers over 40 at least 21 days to review a severance agreement and 7 days to revoke after signing. Workers under 40 typically have a shorter window but rarely just hours.

Do not sign the same day you receive it. Read it carefully — or have an employment attorney review it. Key things to check:

  • What claims are you waiving? Most severance agreements include a release of all legal claims against the company.
  • Is the non-compete reasonable? Non-competes are unenforceable in some states and overly broad in many agreements.
  • Is there anything negotiable? Severance amount, continued health coverage, equity treatment, and reference language are all often negotiable.
Don't touch your 401(k) Early 401(k) withdrawal before age 59½ triggers a 10% penalty plus income tax — effectively losing 30–40% of whatever you take out. Even in a financial emergency, exhaust all other options first. Your 401(k) balance can stay exactly where it is after a layoff.

The rest of week 1

Once the urgent decisions are handled, the rest of week one is about stabilization — not action. Update your budget to survival mode: pause non-essential subscriptions, reduce discretionary spending, and know which bills you'd pause first if you needed to. Set a job search start date — not today, but a specific date a few days out, so you give yourself a moment to recover without losing weeks.

The biggest financial mistake people make after layoffs isn't a single bad decision. It's delay — waiting a week to file unemployment, waiting three weeks to figure out COBRA, waiting a month to understand their runway. The 48-hour window is when the decisions that matter most need to happen.
