How to Negotiate Your Severance (Most People Leave Money on the Table)

The company has already decided to let you go. That means you have more leverage than you think — if you know what to ask for.

LayoffSurvival — July 2026 — 10 min read

The moment you receive a severance offer, most people do one of two things: they sign it immediately because they're in shock, or they refuse it entirely because they're angry. Both are mistakes that cost money.

The truth about severance: it is almost always negotiable to some degree. Companies make initial offers that they expect to be questioned. They've already decided to pay you something — the question is how much. Here's how to approach that conversation.

First: know what you're signing away

Every severance agreement contains a release of claims — meaning you agree not to sue the company for anything related to your employment. This is what the company is paying for. Understanding this changes the negotiation completely: you're not just negotiating pay, you're negotiating the price of releasing your legal rights.

Before you negotiate anything, read the release carefully. If you believe you experienced discrimination, harassment, WARN Act violations, or wage theft, talk to an employment attorney before signing anything. These claims have real dollar value that a larger severance number should reflect.

What is actually negotiable

Companies tend to be flexible on some things and rigid on others. The most negotiable elements:

The severance amount itself

The standard formula is 1–2 weeks of pay per year of service. This is a starting point, not a ceiling. Senior employees, long-tenured workers, and people in specialized roles often receive more. The negotiation is: "I've been here X years and I'm being offered Y weeks — I'd like to discuss whether we can get closer to Z."

What increases your leverage: a strong employment history at the company, evidence that finding a comparable role will take time (senior titles, niche specializations), any legal claims the company might prefer to avoid, and market data showing your role is in demand.

Health insurance continuation

If the company won't increase the cash amount, ask them to pay your COBRA premiums for 1–3 months. At $685–$1,950/month, even one month of paid COBRA is real money. Companies often find this easier to agree to than additional salary continuation because it's a defined benefit with a clear end date.

Equity and unvested stock

If you have unvested RSUs or stock options, ask about accelerated vesting. Companies sometimes agree to vest the next scheduled tranche, especially if it vests within 3–6 months of your departure. This can be worth tens of thousands of dollars for tech employees.

Reference language

Ask for a written confirmation of what the company will say when references are checked. The standard "dates of employment and title only" is fine, but some companies will agree to provide a positive reference. Get this in writing as part of the agreement.

Non-compete terms

If your agreement includes a non-compete, negotiate the scope — duration, geography, and which specific competitors are included. Non-competes are unenforceable in California and several other states, and courts in most states scrutinize overly broad ones. Push back on any non-compete that would genuinely restrict your ability to find work in your field.

What is NOT negotiable The release of claims language itself is usually non-negotiable — the company won't pay severance without it. The confidentiality clause about the layoff process is also typically fixed. Focus your negotiation energy on the financial terms, not the legal boilerplate.

The exact conversation to have

Don't negotiate by email if you can avoid it — phone or video gives you more nuance and builds less of a paper trail. Say something like:

"I want to be reasonable about this. I've been here X years and I've contributed significantly to [specific things]. I'd like to see if we can improve the package before I sign. Specifically, I'm hoping we can discuss the severance amount and whether you'd consider covering my COBRA for a few months. Is there flexibility there?"

Then stop talking. Let them respond. You don't need to justify further — you've made the ask, now you wait for a number.

The 21-day rule if you're over 40

Federal law (OWBPA) gives workers 40 and older at least 21 days to consider a severance agreement, and 7 days to revoke after signing. In group layoffs, this extends to 45 days. Companies are required to tell you this — if they didn't, that's itself a compliance issue worth noting.

Use this time. You don't have to sign on day one. Take a week to review it, have an attorney look at it if you have any concerns, and make your ask. The 21 days is a floor, not a deadline to rush toward.

Don't negotiate emotionally The hardest part of severance negotiation is doing it while you're hurt, angry, or scared. Companies know this and some use it — offering a short window and creating urgency. Take a breath. Sleep on it. Make your ask calmly and in writing if needed. Emotional negotiations rarely improve outcomes.
