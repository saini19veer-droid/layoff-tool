COBRA vs ACA After a Layoff: The Real Cost Comparison

This is the decision most people get wrong — either paying too much for COBRA they didn't need, or going uninsured when they shouldn't. Here's the math.

LayoffSurvival — July 2026 — 9 min read

Health insurance is the most expensive and most confusing financial decision you face after a layoff. It's also the one with the hardest deadline — you have 60 days from your layoff to make your choice, and getting it wrong in either direction is costly.

COBRA lets you stay on your employer's plan. The ACA marketplace gives you subsidized alternatives. Here's how to figure out which one is right for your situation.

What COBRA actually costs in 2026

When you were employed, your employer was paying most of your health insurance premium — often 70–80% of the total cost. COBRA makes you pay 100% of that full premium, plus a 2% administrative fee. The result is often shocking.

On an annual basis, family COBRA costs nearly $23,400. That's a major drain on your financial runway — the single largest new expense most laid-off workers face.

What ACA marketplace plans cost after a layoff

A job loss is a qualifying life event that lets you enroll in an ACA marketplace plan outside the regular open enrollment period. You have 60 days from your last day of employer coverage to enroll.

The key factor is income-based subsidies. ACA subsidies are calculated based on your projected annual income for the year — and after a layoff, your income for the year drops significantly. Lower income means larger subsidies, which means lower premiums.

When COBRA is the right choice

Despite the cost, COBRA is sometimes the better option:

  • You've already met your deductible for the year. Switching plans resets your deductible to zero. If it's October and you've hit your $3,000 deductible, staying on COBRA through December protects that.
  • You're mid-treatment for something ongoing — cancer treatment, pregnancy, ongoing specialist care. Switching plans risks network disruption or coverage gaps.
  • Your income stays high — if you have significant investment income or a spouse with high income, ACA subsidies may be small or zero, making COBRA cost-competitive.
  • You expect to be re-employed quickly — if you have a new job starting in 6 weeks, the administrative simplicity of COBRA (staying on your existing plan) may be worth more than a few hundred dollars in savings.
The deductible math If it's mid-year and you've already paid $2,500 toward a $3,000 deductible on COBRA, an ACA plan that costs $400/month less would need to be cheaper by at least $2,500 to break even before year-end — which means it would need to cost $400+ less per month AND you'd need to have zero medical expenses for the rest of the year. Do this math for your specific situation before switching.

The 60-day window and what happens if you miss it

You have 60 days from losing employer coverage to enroll in an ACA plan (your layoff counts as a qualifying life event). Miss that window and you're locked out of ACA enrollment until the next open enrollment period in November — leaving you uninsured for potentially months.

You also have 60 days to elect COBRA. If you want to compare ACA options first and then decide, you can — but elect COBRA first to preserve the option, because COBRA coverage is retroactive to the day your employer coverage ended. If you elect ACA and then have a medical emergency, COBRA retroactive election is your safety net.

How to compare your specific options

Go to healthcare.gov and enter your projected income for this year — your actual income earned so far plus any expected income for the rest of the year (unemployment benefits count as income for ACA purposes). The site will show you available plans and your subsidy amount.

Compare the total cost including premiums, deductibles, and whether your doctors and medications are covered. Then compare that to your COBRA quote from your former employer's HR department.
